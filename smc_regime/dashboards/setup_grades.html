Setup Grades

Every tracked ticker's A–D setup grade, scored on the chart itself: trend structure against the 50/200 MA, RSI read against the regime, MACD momentum, and volume conviction — plus regime stability, timeframe and peer alignment, and valuation stretch. Which strategy to trade it with is reported, not scored — that question only matters once the setup is worth taking.

Scored purely from the last nightly snapshot — RSI/MACD/volume/50–200 MA readings from technicals (captured off each ticker's final bar during the nightly run), regime/streak/peer directions from latest_regime, forward/trailing P/E from Yahoo. No live data fetched at export time. Component weights (trend structure 20 / RSI 15 / MACD 15 / volume conviction 15 / regime streak 10 / daily–weekly alignment 10 / sector&industry alignment 5 / valuation 10 — split 6 for the absolute forward multiple, 4 for its direction against trailing, that direction faded toward neutral when the trailing multiple is too high for the ratio to mean anything) are documented defaults in smc_regime/setup_score.py, not fixed. Grade thresholds (A ≥ 76, B ≥ 68, C ≥ 57) were calibrated against this universe's actual spread on the first run carrying real technical readings — scores ran 40–90, mean 64 — targeting roughly A 10% / B 25% / C 40% / D 25%. They describe this universe, so re-check them if the tracking list or the weights change materially. Scores bunch tightly near those cuts — the A band alone averages under 0.4 points between adjacent names — so anything within 1.0 point of a boundary carries a dashed ~X tag naming the grade on the other side: a B tagged ~A fell a fraction short and is effectively an A, an A tagged ~B only just holds it. Read the score, not the letter, on those. RSI is read against the regime: a pullback into the 30s inside a trend is an entry, the same reading at the top of a choppy range is not. Strategy edge from db.best_strategy() is shown as information only and contributes zero points, and so do the two chips on each row. Headline tone (▲▲ much stronger / ▲ stronger / ▼ weaker / ▼▼ much weaker than this feed's norm; typical gets no chip) is a VADER compound score over the last week of Tiingo headlines, extended with a small finance lexicon. Its bands are percentiles of this feed's own spread, not VADER's generic thresholds: those put 95% of the universe in a single band on the first live run, because ordinary newswire language scores positive (“names new CFO” is +0.48) and this feed's median headline sits at +0.34. The labels say weaker and stronger rather than negative and positive for the same reason — calling a +0.15 ticker negative would invent a claim the score does not make. It measures the language a headline chose and nothing else — it cannot tell a writedown from a reporter reaching for “plunges”, and it does not know how big the number was. ER nd counts calendar days to the next earnings date from Yahoo, chipped only inside three weeks, dashed while Yahoo still calls that date an estimate. Both are stored as raw facts — a date, a timestamp — and counted against your own clock when the page loads, so they stay right however long after the export you open it. Neither moves a grade: a name reporting on Thursday is the same setup as one reporting in six weeks, carrying a different risk. A third chip reads nth vol, because sentiment is the one thing on this page that cannot check itself: it reads the words a headline chose and has no idea whether anyone traded on the story. Volume is the nearest available materiality check — the last 5 bars' average against the last 50 — but it is reported as a percentile of that ticker's own history, not as a multiple. A fixed threshold asks a different question of every name: across this universe the ratio marking a ticker's own 95th percentile runs from 1.40× to 4.72×, and 14% of names never reach 1.5× at all, so “1.5×” is unreachable for PG and KO while being an ordinary week for QUBT. Chipped at the 95th percentile and above, or the 5th and below. The expanded row leads with volume and uses the headlines to explain it, which is the causal order — a spike is evidence something happened, the tone is a guess at what — and the cell worth looking for is heavy volume with nothing written about it. The two stay separate readings: nothing here establishes that a given spike and a given headline are connected. The small figure beside each grade is how long that ticker has held it — 9d means the grade has read the same since then. Like the earnings countdown, only the date is stored and the day count is worked out against your clock when the page loads, so it stays right however long after the export you open it. Two things would otherwise inflate it. A snapshot that runs on a weekend or a market holiday re-files the previous session's closes under a new date, which is not another day at that grade — consecutive captures with an identical close collapse to one, and this is not hypothetical: a run dispatched on a Saturday night repeated Friday's closes for all 415 tickers and would have added a phantom day to every row on this page. A missed run, by contrast, does not break a streak: if the grade reads the same either side of a skipped night, it carries through, since resetting on every scheduler hiccup would be wrong far more often. Neither does an isolated one-capture flip. Scores bunch within a point of the grade cuts — which is what the ~A tags are about — so a name sitting a fraction from a boundary changes grade on noise, and a strict streak ends up reporting days since the last wobble rather than how settled the grade is. On the first history deep enough to test, every one of 415 tickers had its current grade interrupted at least once, and a quarter of the board read a streak of one. So a single capture with the same grade either side is crossed rather than counted as a break; two in a row is a real change and does break it, and so does a disagreement at the very edge of the history, which has nothing before it to confirm continuity. Chips that crossed a flip carry a dashed underline and name the date and the unbroken run in their tooltip, alongside the plain ratio — “7 of the last 8 captures at grade C” — because a streak that quietly swallowed a reversal would be exactly the false confidence the strict count was free of. A trailing + means the streak reaches the start of the captured history and is therefore a floor, not a measurement — grade capture began on 1 September, so 46 of 415 tickers are currently at that boundary and may have held their grade far longer than shown. The macro strip under the grade counts is context and nothing more — no figure in it touches a grade. Levels (10Y, WTI, Brent, gold, dollar index, VIX, and the S&P/Nasdaq benchmarks) come from Yahoo's chart endpoint, so they are a snapshot from when the page was built, never live: this page cannot fetch anything when you open it, because the artifact sandbox blocks outbound requests. That is fine for a yield or a crude print and actively misleading for overnight futures, which is why the strip always states how old it is. The two equity benchmarks switch by session: during the cash day you get S&P 500 and Nasdaq 100, the indices everything on this page is actually measured against; overnight and pre-market the cash indices are frozen at yesterday's close, so the strip falls back to S&P futures and Nasdaq futures and says so in the label rather than printing a stale index. The switch is decided by whether the index has traded that day, not by the clock, so market holidays and half-days fall out correctly without a holiday calendar. Event chips work the opposite way round: the date is stored and the countdown is computed against your own clock as the page loads, so “in 3d” stays right however long after the export you open it. Rate decisions, inflation and jobs prints carry a dot and heavier type because they reprice the whole tape; a housing-starts number does not. The schedule is a committed file (smc_regime/data/macro_calendar.json) rather than a live feed, because the nightly runs in GitHub Actions where the calendar sources are unreachable — it reaches about a month ahead and needs refreshing monthly. An expired calendar would otherwise render an empty week that reads like calm, so the strip says so outright when its own end date has passed.
